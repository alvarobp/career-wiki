# Johari Window

**Source:** [Why other people can see things about you that you can't](https://buttondown.com/untools/archive/why-other-people-can-see-things-about-you-that/) - Tobe (Untools)

## Summary

Most people know themselves less well than they think. Tasha Eurich's research on self-awareness, which covered thousands of people, found that only **10–15% are genuinely self-aware**. Everyone else has **blind spots**: patterns that other people see clearly and that the person can't see at all.

The **Johari Window** is a framework from the 1950s for finding those blind spots. It sorts everything about you into four quadrants, depending on whether you know it and whether others know it. The main lesson is that **most personal growth comes from shrinking the Blind Spot**, which means finding out what others already see. You do that by asking for feedback deliberately and taking it in well.

## The Four Quadrants

|                        | **Known to others** | **Hidden from others** |
|------------------------|---------------------|------------------------|
| **Known to you**       | Open Arena          | Façade                 |
| **Not known to you**   | Blind Spot          | Unknown                |

- **Open Arena**: known to you and to others. This covers your strengths, your habits and the obvious things about you.
- **Blind Spot**: known to others but not to you. It's the thing everyone notices except you.
- **Façade**: known to you but hidden from others. These are the things you keep private.
- **Unknown**: known to no one, including you. This holds untapped potential and triggers you haven't discovered yet.

## Why the Blind Spot Matters Most

- Other people already have this information. The only thing missing is that it hasn't reached you.
- Having a blind spot doesn't mean you've been dishonest about yourself. **You just didn't have the data.**
- Once you get the information, the trait moves into the **Open Arena**. Only then can you work on it.

### Example

You think of yourself as a great listener, and it feels true, so you'd put it in your Open Arena. Then three colleagues, in three separate reviews, say you jump in with solutions before people finish talking. That is a Blind Spot. Because several people said the same thing, you can trust it. It now moves into the Open Arena, and you can do something about it.

## How to Shrink Your Blind Spots

- **Ask 2–3 people you trust:** "What's something I do that I might not be aware of?"
- **Look for patterns across feedback.** One comment may be a one-off. When several people say the same thing, the signal is much stronger.
- **Notice your reaction.** Feedback can sting, but stay open to it. Growth often comes from discomfort.
- **Time it well.** Don't look for blind spots right before a high-stakes moment such as a performance review or a big presentation. Ask when you have time to think about the answer. Under pressure you'll get defensive instead of curious.

## Core Insight

You can't find your blind spots by thinking harder about yourself. By definition, the information sits with other people. Self-awareness therefore depends on asking for feedback on purpose. Ask several trusted people, trust the patterns more than any single comment, treat the sting as a sign you're learning something, and ask at a calm time so you can respond with curiosity rather than defensiveness.
